Sentiment Analysis on
Restaurant Reviews

An NLP project investigating whether the sentiment expressed in restaurant reviews actually predicts the star rating — and whether the day of the week or review length play a role too.

What this project does
⭐
The question

When someone leaves a 5-star review, does their language actually sound more positive? And can we predict the rating from the sentiment alone — or do factors like the day of the week matter too?

🧹
The approach

Restaurant reviews were cleaned and standardised using NLP techniques, then scored for sentiment using two independent libraries (sentimentr and qdap). The scores were compared and used as predictors in regression models.

📊
The finding

Sentiment is a significant predictor of rating (coefficient 0.9), but the day of the week has no effect. Interestingly, 5-star reviews have both very positive and very negative outliers — people feel strongly either way.

How it works
01
Text preprocessing

Raw review text was cleaned in 6 steps: lowercasing, punctuation removal, number removal, whitespace stripping, stopword removal, and stemming. Each step removes noise that would confuse the sentiment algorithm — words like "the", "and", "of" carry no sentiment signal.

02
Dual sentiment scoring

Two different NLP libraries were used independently — sentimentr and qdap — and their scores compared. The correlation between the two was 0.71, confirming they largely agree. This cross-validation approach adds reliability to the sentiment scores.

03
Exploratory analysis

Before modelling, we visualised the relationship between sentiment and rating with boxplots and bar charts by year. Higher-rated restaurants showed consistently higher polarity — but also more variance. Longer reviews slightly correlated with lower ratings (r = -0.11).

04
Fixed-effects regression

A fixed-effects regression (feols) was used to control for restaurant-level differences and date factors. This isolates the pure effect of sentiment on rating, removing the influence of a restaurant always getting high or low scores regardless of review content.

05
Key findings

Sentiment score is highly significant (p < 0.05) with a coefficient of 0.9 — meaning a 1-unit increase in polarity increases the predicted rating by ~0.9 stars. Day of the week is not significant. Review length is significant in the full model, suggesting shorter reviews tend to be more positive.

Key results
Finding 1
Sentiment predicts rating. For every 1-unit increase in polarity score, the rating increases by approximately 0.9 stars (p < 0.05). The relationship holds even after controlling for restaurant identity and review date.
Finding 2
Day of the week doesn't matter. Despite intuition suggesting weekend reviewers might be more generous, the day of the week has essentially zero effect (coefficient 0.002, not significant).
Finding 3
5-star reviews are emotionally extreme. Unlike 4-star reviews which show mostly positive sentiment, 5-star reviews have a wide spread of outliers — both very positive and surprisingly negative. People who leave top ratings may be expressing complex emotions.
Technical deep dive

A walkthrough of the key code sections. Full code available on GitHub.

01 — Text preprocessing pipelineR
# Step 1: lowercase RestaurantReviews$review_full <- tolower(RestaurantReviews$review_full) # Step 2: remove punctuation RestaurantReviews$review_full <- removePunctuation(RestaurantReviews$review_full) # Step 3: remove numbers RestaurantReviews$review_full <- removeNumbers(RestaurantReviews$review_full) # Step 4: strip whitespace RestaurantReviews$review_full <- stripWhitespace(RestaurantReviews$review_full) # Step 5: remove stopwords (the, and, of, ...) RestaurantReviews$review_full <- removeWords(RestaurantReviews$review_full, stopwords("english")) # Step 6: stemming (running → run, amazing → amaz) RestaurantReviews$review_full <- stemDocument(RestaurantReviews$review_full, language = "english")
Each step removes a specific type of noise. Stemming is particularly important — it reduces words to their root form so "amazing", "amazingly", and "amazed" all become "amaz" and are treated as the same signal by the sentiment algorithm.
02 — Dual sentiment scoringR
# Library 1: sentimentr sentiment_scores <- sentiment(RestaurantReviews$review_full) RestaurantReviews$sentiment_score <- sentiment_scores$sentiment # Library 2: qdap (polarity-based) polarity_score <- polarity(RestaurantReviews$review_full) RestaurantReviews$polarity_score <- as.numeric(counts(polarity_score)[,3]) # Cross-validate: correlation between the two scores cor(RestaurantReviews$polarity_score, RestaurantReviews$sentiment_score) # Result: 0.71 — strong agreement between libraries
Using two independent libraries and checking their correlation (0.71) is a form of triangulation — if two different methods agree, we can be more confident the scores are capturing real signal rather than library-specific artefacts.
03 — Fixed-effects regression modelR
library(fixest) # Model 1: sentiment as predictor, restaurant + date as fixed effects model <- feols( rating_review ~ sentiment_score_review_full | restaurant_name + day + month + year, data = RestaurantReviews ) # Model 2: add day of the week as explicit predictor model_day <- feols( rating_review ~ sentiment_score_review_full + day | restaurant_name + month + year, data = RestaurantReviews ) # Result: sentiment coeff = 0.90 (p < 0.05), day coeff = 0.002 (not significant)
Fixed-effects regression controls for characteristics that don't change — like a restaurant always attracting high-end customers. By absorbing restaurant identity as a fixed effect, we isolate the pure relationship between sentiment and rating, making the estimate more reliable.
